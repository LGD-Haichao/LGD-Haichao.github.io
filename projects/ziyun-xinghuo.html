A lab can build a better model. That does not mean a clinician will trust it.

Context

The project grew out of a university lab working on computer-assisted knee-ligament surgery navigation. The technical problem is hard enough — CT and MRI images differ in resolution, contrast, and the structures they show, so a model trained on one does not simply transfer to the other. The human problem is harder: a surgeon adopts a tool only when it fits the existing workflow, explains its uncertainty, and saves time rather than adding a screen.

Our team spent the summer of 2026 in Beijing and Hefei, talking to the people on both sides of that gap: clinicians, imaging staff, community residents, and a medical-device manufacturer.

My role

I was the documentation and communications lead. I co-authored the 10,000-word project plan and the separate interview guides for clinicians and residents, kept the written record coherent across 334 interviews, and helped synthesize the evidence into the team's deliverables. I was also one of two speakers at the key-project approval defense.

How I worked

Design for two audiences, not one

The clinician guide asked about decision-making, trust, and failure modes. The community-resident guide asked about access, comprehension, and the questions people actually ask outside a clinic. Keeping the two guides separate prevented us from averaging a department head's priorities with a resident's.

Make 334 interviews usable

Scale is only useful if the record is consistent. We used a shared note structure — context, observed workflow, pain point, quote, follow-up — so that 334 conversations could be read as a dataset rather than a pile of transcripts. I owned the synthesis: clustering needs, separating frequent complaints from high-consequence ones, and writing the narrative that connected them.

Translate the lab's work into clinical language

The project plan had to explain cross-modal registration, segmentation, and navigation without assuming a computer-science reader. I rewrote the technical sections around clinical consequences: what the surgeon sees, what the system does when the image is poor, and what happens when the system is not sure.

Build a public-facing layer

The team launched a project website with an AI Q&A assistant so that the work was not locked inside a PDF. I contributed the written structure and the public narrative; the engineering team built the site. The project also produced 3 co-construction agreements and 4 letters of appreciation from partner organizations.

Outcomes

  • University 1st Prize for social practice.
  • 334 valid interviews across communities, a medtech manufacturer, and two hospitals.
  • 3 co-construction agreements and 4 letters of appreciation.
  • Two policy reports from the wider project were adopted at different levels (team outcome).
  • Research-report and outstanding-individual awards were still under review when the project closed.

What I learned

In a clinical setting, “the model is 96% accurate” is not an answer. The useful questions are: accurate on whom, under what image quality, and what does the interface do when it is wrong? I became much more careful about the difference between a metric and a decision — and much better at writing technical work for people who will never read the paper.

Credits

Team led by Wu Runbao; faculty advisors Zhao Yichao and Liu Bin. The interviews and agreements were collective; the written synthesis and defense preparation were my main contribution.
