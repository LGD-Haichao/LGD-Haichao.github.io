Mapping the talent gap in Liaoning's integrated-circuit industry

Liaoning trains a lot of engineers. Keeping them is a different problem.

Context

Shenyang is home to a dense cluster of integrated-circuit (IC) equipment makers, state-owned aerospace institutes, and research universities. The 601 Aircraft Design Institute, Kingsemi (Xinyuan Microelectronics), and a growing semiconductor supply chain all sit within a short drive of one another. Yet the region struggles to retain the software, electronics, and materials graduates it educates.

The gap is not only about salary. Students often do not know what the local industry actually does; companies struggle to explain their needs in the language of a twenty-year-old; policy incentives exist but rarely reach the people deciding where to work. The team's question was deliberately practical: where does the talent pipeline leak between university, first job, and five-year retention — and what can a local government service center do about it?

My role

I was the research and documentation lead (宣传组组长) for the winter social-practice team from the School of Integrated Circuits. I joined the stakeholder interviews, owned the written record, and turned it into outputs different audiences could use: a research report, a policy brief, six WeChat articles, and two news releases. I also helped the team package two Dengpan Cup entries.

How I worked

1. Map the stakeholders before the questions

We split the field into four groups: government (Shenyang human resources and social insurance agencies, the provincial employment service center), enterprises (601 and Kingsemi), universities and students, and public venues (Liaoning Science and Technology Museum, China Industrial Museum). Each group received a different interview guide — a policy actor cares about uptake, a process engineer cares about skills, a student cares about the first three years.

2. Collect both breadth and texture

  • 81 valid survey responses from students and young professionals on industry awareness, job preferences, and information channels.
  • Site visits and semi-structured interviews at 601, Kingsemi, the provincial science museum, the industrial museum, and municipal HR agencies.
  • About 40,000 characters of handwritten field notes, typed up the same evening so that quotes and context stayed attached.

3. Synthesize into a pipeline, not a list

I organized the evidence as a pipeline — how students hear about the industry, what universities teach, how firms hire, and why people stay — and marked the policy touchpoints at each stage. That structure made the recommendations legible to a service center that could only act on a few of them; the brief concentrated on the parts a municipal actor could actually move.

4. Write for four different readers

The same fieldwork became a formal research report, a short policy brief, six public articles, and two news releases. The brief had to be precise; the WeChat posts had to be readable; the news pieces had to be publishable. Writing the same material four ways was the most useful writing exercise I have done.

Outcomes

  • The policy brief was adopted by the Shenyang Social Insurance Service Center in 2026.
  • Team awards: University 2nd Prize for social practice, Outstanding Research Report 3rd Prize; personally named Outstanding Individual 3rd Prize.
  • Public communication: six WeChat articles and two news releases published by China Daily (Chinese-language site) and China.com.cn.
  • Separately, I helped package two Dengpan Cup entries: a Dagon College 2nd Prize and a School of Integrated Circuits 3rd Prize.

What I learned

The useful part was structured listening. The value was not in collecting more interviews; it was in deciding which four stages mattered, which two a municipal actor could actually move, and which sentence a busy official would read first. I also learned that a policy document and a WeChat post are not the same argument in different fonts — they are different arguments for different readers.

The research report and policy brief are internal documents. A redacted summary is available on request; the public articles are linked in the sidebar.

Credits

Team: a cross-disciplinary group from the School of Integrated Circuits, led by the project team lead; faculty advisor: Song Kuo. The policy brief and public articles were my own writing; the fieldwork was collective.
