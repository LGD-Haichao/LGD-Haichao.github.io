About

A software engineer who likes the messy part.

I grew up in Shenyang and study software engineering in Dalian. I like problems where the technical part is only half of it — the other half is figuring out what people actually need.

Why research, why AI

My first serious project was a field study on why Liaoning struggles to keep integrated-circuit talent. I expected the answer to be salary. It was not. Students did not know what the local industry did; companies could not explain their needs; policy incentives existed but never reached the people deciding where to work. That project taught me that the hard part is not the analysis; it is figuring out which decision the analysis is supposed to help.

I ended up in AI for a similar reason. A model that says “96% accurate” is not useful to a low-vision user reading a medication label. What matters is whether the system can show where it got the answer, say “I am not sure,” and stop when the image is unreadable. That is why my current project is a reading assistant that checks itself and refuses to guess — not another chatbot.

At DLUT I have also run a league branch, organized 60+ volunteer events, led a 15-person alumni-outreach team, and taught high-school chemistry for two weeks in a rural town. Those experiences are not a detour from the technical work; they are where I learned to coordinate people, write for different readers, and do what I said I would.

What I care about

  • Show your work. If a claim cannot be traced to a quote, a number, or a document, it should not be in the report.
  • Assume less. A tool that only works for a confident, sighted, native-language user is not finished.
  • Public service as a habit. I have been a volunteer guide at the Liaoning Provincial Museum since 2012; explaining an object to a stranger is a good antidote to jargon.
